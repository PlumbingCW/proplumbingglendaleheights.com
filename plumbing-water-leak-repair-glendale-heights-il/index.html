Plumbing Water Leak Repair for Glendale Heights, IL Homes

To confirm a plumbing water leak, turn off every fixture and watch your water meter. If the dial or the small leak indicator keeps moving, water is escaping somewhere in your lines. Shut the main if the leak is active and call a plumber to trace it. The repair then depends on the pipe, its location and the damage around it.

This guide gives the home tests, the usual hiding places and what to expect from a repair.

By the Glendale Heights Pro Plumbing team · Published 2026-11-16 · Updated 2026-11-16

Three tests you can do today

The meter test is the best. Close every tap, stop the washer and dishwasher and look at the meter. A moving dial or a spinning leak indicator means water is flowing. Write down the reading, wait an hour with everything off, and read it again. A changed reading confirms a leak.

The toilet dye test finds a silent leak. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait fifteen minutes without flushing. Color in the bowl means the flapper leaks. The third test is a walk-through. Check under every sink, around the water heater, at the washer hoses and along basement ceilings.

A leak that stains a ceiling below a bathroom is often at a toilet seal, a tub drain or a supply connection. A stain that appears only after heavy rain points to the roof or the foundation, not plumbing.

What a plumber does

A plumber confirms the leak with the meter and a pressure test, then narrows it with listening gear and a thermal camera. Only when the source is known does anyone cut a wall. The repair is then aimed. It might replace a short section of pipe, a fitting or a valve, or it might re-route a failing line.

If the same line has leaked more than once, ask about replacing the whole run. Pinholes in one place often mean thin pipe elsewhere, and a partial repair will not hold long.

Why the same leak keeps coming back

A repair that fails within a year usually means the pipe around it is also thin. Copper that pinholes in one spot is often corroding along a longer run. A fitting that weeps again may have been fixed without cleaning and re-seating the joint.

Ask the plumber what caused the first failure and whether the rest of the run is sound. Water pressure plays a part too. Pressure above the comfortable range stresses every joint and every appliance valve. A simple gauge on a hose bib shows the number in a minute.

If it reads high, a pressure reducing valve is a modest fix that protects the pipes you already own.

Cost without surprises

The detection visit is priced on its own, so you know the cause before you approve repair. The repair price depends on pipe material, access and the finish work to close the wall. Drywall, paint and flooring are often separate from the plumbing price, so ask what is included. Small leaks cost more later.

Water damages framing and insulation and feeds mold. A leak found early costs less to repair and less to dry out.

Glendale Heights facts for leak owners

The Village's 2024 water quality report says that lead in drinking water mainly comes from service lines and home plumbing, and it advises flushing taps for several minutes after water has sat unused. It lists Public Works at (630) 260-6040 as the contact for testing questions.

A leak on the pipe between the street and your meter is a utility matter, while a leak after the meter is yours, and the Public Works number is the place to confirm where the line changes hands at your address.

The Village's brochures list Community Development at (630) 260-6030 for permit questions on repair work that goes beyond a simple fix.

What to do while you wait

Shut the main if water is spreading. Move belongings out of the wet area. Take dated photos for your insurer. Do not cut into a wall to hunt for the leak, because that can spread damage and make the leak harder to find. Turn off the power to a wet area only from dry ground.

If you are unsure, wait for the plumber.

Insurance

Many policies cover water damage from a sudden leak but not the cost of finding or repairing a slow one. Read your policy, call your insurer early and keep photos and receipts. Ask whether the policy covers tear-out and access, which varies.
